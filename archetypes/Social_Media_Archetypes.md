### 1. Joe Blah — The Ghost

Joe is 52, married, works in IT, and has three grown children. He joined Facebook years ago because everyone else did and because his wife kept sending him invitations. His profile picture is from 2018, and his bio still says, “Just enjoying life!”

Joe logs in almost every day, but mostly just scrolls. He reads posts from friends, looks at vacation pictures, watches whatever videos the algorithm puts in front of him, and occasionally checks what his kids are up to. He almost never posts anything himself.

He doesn't react to posts, either. His friends could announce a wedding, a new baby, or a serious medical diagnosis and Joe would probably read it without clicking Like. He isn't antisocial; he simply has no instinct to *participate* in social media. When he sees someone in person, he'll say, “Yeah, I saw that on Facebook,” proving that he was paying attention all along.

Joe represents the enormous population of passive users who provide an audience without contributing much visible activity.

### 2. Mary Jane — The Living Her Best Life Influencer

Mary is 34, divorced, has two kids, works in sales, and seems to have a social calendar that would exhaust a professional athlete.

Her feed is a constant stream of parties, cocktails, concerts, brunches, weekend getaways, selfies, and pictures with friends. Every photograph looks spontaneous, although it somehow always seems to have been carefully staged. She posts frequently, sometimes several times a day, and pays close attention to reactions.

Mary has mastered the art of posting things that are just provocative enough to generate comments without quite crossing the line. Her friends occasionally worry about what her boss might think, particularly because her boss is also one of her followers.

She doesn't merely use social media to document her life. She uses it to *present* a version of her life.

When people comment, Mary responds enthusiastically. She remembers birthdays, tags people in old photos, and is quick with compliments. She genuinely enjoys the social feedback loop.

### 3. Grandma Dorothy — The Forwarding Machine

Dorothy is 79, widowed, retired, and has discovered that the internet is full of important information that television somehow isn't reporting.

She has hundreds of friends, many of whom she has never met. She posts pictures of her cat, photographs of her grandchildren, Bible verses, inspirational sayings, warnings about scams, recipes, patriotic messages, and alarming stories about things that supposedly happened “yesterday.”

Much of what she shares is false.

Dorothy isn't trying to spread misinformation. She sincerely believes she's helping people. When someone points out that an article is a hoax, she is genuinely surprised and occasionally offended. Five minutes later, however, she'll find another article making an equally dubious claim and share that too.

She also photographs framed family photographs hanging on her wall. Unfortunately, every picture has glare from the glass, making the people in it nearly impossible to identify.

Her social media activity is driven by connection and concern. She sees sharing something with you as an act of friendship.

### 4. Jim Roxx — The Hobby Evangelist

Jim is 61, divorced, works as a machinist, rides motorcycles, and plays lead guitar in a classic-rock cover band called *The Afterburners*.

Jim's social media feed has exactly four subjects: motorcycles, classic rock, motorcycles *and* classic rock together, and whatever his band is doing this weekend.

He posts pictures of his motorcycle from every conceivable angle. If he buys a new exhaust system, everyone knows about it. If he changes his handlebars, everyone knows about it. If he goes on a 150-mile ride, there will be photographs.

When the band has a gig, Jim posts the flyer six times in three different versions and personally tags everyone who might possibly attend.

Jim isn't particularly interested in politics, viral trends, influencers, or whatever everybody else is talking about. He has found his thing, and social media is primarily a way to share that thing with people who might appreciate it.

His friends sometimes tease him about his obsessive posting, but they also know exactly where to find him on a Saturday night.

### 5. Karen Commenter — The Opinion Department

Karen is 46, divorced, has two teenagers, manages a medical office, and has never encountered an opinion she felt comfortable leaving unanswered.

Karen doesn't necessarily post a lot of original material. Her real talent is commenting.

Someone posts a photograph of a restaurant and Karen has an opinion about the service. Someone posts an article about taxes and Karen has an opinion about that. Someone mentions a television show she doesn't watch and Karen somehow has an opinion about it.

She is particularly attracted to posts involving politics, parenting, schools, crime, celebrities, or anything beginning with “I can't believe this...”

Karen can turn an innocent comment thread into a 47-response argument without ever technically insulting anyone. She frequently writes things like “I'm just saying...” immediately before saying something guaranteed to start an argument.

She isn't a troll. That's what makes her different. She sincerely believes she's participating in meaningful conversation.

### 6. Tyler — The Professional Content Creator Who Isn't Actually a Professional

Tyler is 24, lives with two roommates, works part-time at a coffee shop, and has decided that his real career is going to be “content creation.”

His social media accounts are a carefully constructed ecosystem of short videos, memes, reaction clips, photographs, personal observations, product recommendations, and occasional attempts at going viral.

Tyler follows trends obsessively. He knows what audio clips are popular, what hashtags are supposedly effective, and how long a video should be before viewers lose interest. He deletes posts that don't get enough engagement.

Whenever something happens in his life, his first thought isn't necessarily “What do I think about this?” It is “Could I make content out of this?”

He desperately wants to become an influencer, but at the moment his biggest sponsorship has been a free phone case.

His friends have learned that hanging out with Tyler may result in accidentally appearing in somebody else's social-media content.

### 7. Susan — The Family Historian

Susan is 58, married, works as an elementary-school administrator, and has become the unofficial archivist of her entire extended family.

She photographs everything.

Birthdays. Graduations. Christmas dinners. Family reunions. Trips to the beach. Cousins who haven't seen each other in twelve years. Dogs wearing Santa hats.

Susan is constantly tagging relatives and creating albums. She remembers exactly who was at a particular event and will correct you if you misidentify the year.

Her posts aren't particularly clever, controversial, or entertaining. They are a running documentary of family life.

She also loves posting old photographs with captions like, “This popped up in my memories today!” followed by a picture of someone who was 30 years younger and several people who are now dead.

Susan's relationship with social media is fundamentally archival. She isn't trying to build a personal brand. She's building a family history.

### 8. Dave Crypto — The Guy Who Has Figured It All Out

Dave is 39, works in construction, has three kids, and discovered cryptocurrency during the pandemic.

Since then, he has become an expert on cryptocurrency, government conspiracies, economic collapse, artificial intelligence, the Federal Reserve, and things that apparently “they” don't want ordinary people to know.

His posts frequently contain screenshots, charts, videos with dramatic music, and statements beginning with “WAKE UP.”

Dave believes conventional news sources are unreliable, but he will enthusiastically share a video made by an anonymous man sitting in a basement because “this guy has done his research.”

He argues with strangers more than he talks to his actual friends. He also predicts that some enormous event is “about to happen” approximately every three weeks.

Despite the apparent chaos, Dave is remarkably consistent: social media has given him a community of people who share his worldview, and he enjoys being one of the people who knows “what's really going on.”

### 9. Lisa — The Perfect-Life Poster

Lisa is 42, married, has two children, owns a nice house, and works part-time as a real-estate agent.

Her social-media feed looks like an advertisement for a lifestyle magazine.

Her children are always clean and smiling. Her meals are beautifully arranged. Her vacations involve sunsets. Her house is spotless. Her husband is apparently delighted to participate in spontaneous family photos.

Lisa posts inspirational quotes about gratitude, carefully selected photographs of her morning coffee, before-and-after home projects, and pictures of family outings that somehow never include anyone looking tired, annoyed, or bored.

Her real life is probably considerably more ordinary than her feed suggests.

She doesn't necessarily consider herself an influencer. She simply likes presenting a positive version of her life. Social media is partly a scrapbook and partly a mirror in which she hopes to see the person she wants to be.

### 10. Mike — The Friend Collector

Mike is 31, single, works in software, and has 4,800 “friends.”

He met some of them once at a conference. Others went to high school with his cousin. Some are people he knows from gaming communities. A few he has never met at all.

Mike accepts nearly every legitimate friend request.

His feed is therefore an astonishing mixture of unrelated lives. He sees political arguments, baby pictures, vacation photos, gaming clips, workout videos, restaurant recommendations, wedding announcements, memes, cryptocurrency speculation, and photographs of people's lunches.

Mike rarely posts anything deeply personal. Instead, he uses the platform almost like a giant social bulletin board. He likes things, shares memes, responds to birthday posts, occasionally sends somebody a private message, and somehow maintains hundreds of weak social connections.

He doesn't necessarily know most of these people well.

That's precisely what he likes about it.

For Mike, social media isn't really a substitute for friendship. It's a way of maintaining a huge network of people who might otherwise disappear from his life completely.
